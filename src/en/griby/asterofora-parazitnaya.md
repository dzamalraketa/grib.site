## Description

Cap 1–2 cm, miniature, greyish-white with a characteristically **silky-fibrous** surface — hence "silky". Gills sparse, whitish, later dusted by a **powdery mass of chlamydospores** — the fungus switches to asexual reproduction and the cap seems to "powder itself". Stem 1–3 cm, slender, curved; at its base — a **brown fragment of the decaying host mushroom**: the whole fungus is embedded in the flesh of a brittlegill or milk cap. Spore print white (sexual spores) plus a brown mass of resting chlamydospores.

## A mushroom on a mushroom — parasite number three

*A. parasitica* completes the parasite trio of our catalogue: Pseudoboletus grows on the earthball, Tremella on Peniophora, and Asterophora — **on the fruit bodies of ordinary edible mushrooms**. Hosts are mainly old, rotting *Russula* and *Lactarius*: the fungus spears the dying neighbour's tissue and finishes it off, raising its own tiny caps. Alongside sexual spores it produces thick-walled **chlamydospores** — a survival reserve.

## How to find it

Look for **decaying** brittlegills and milk caps: old, soft, darkened fruit bodies. If grey miniature caps poke out of such "rot", it is Asterophora. Appearances are sporadic: they track the "harvest" of the hosts.

## Edibility

Inedible — microscopic size plus a "rotten mushroom" substrate make the question moot. Its value is ecological and spectacular: one of the few agaric mycoparasites visible to the naked eye.

## Lookalikes

- **Asterophora lycoperdoides** (star piggyback): the North American relative, larger, with starry cap scales; also on rotting agarics.
- **Young Mycena and Collybia** on the same spot: those grow **from soil or litter**, not from a mushroom's body.
