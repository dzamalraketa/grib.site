## Description

Cap 0.5–2 cm, bell-shaped → convex, **brownish, fading on drying** (hygrophanous — it "washes out"), sometimes with a wavy edge. Gills adnate-toothed, cream → brownish. Stem thin (2–4 cm), brown, with a **white felty "mouse-tail"** at the base — hence myosura ("mouse tail"). The fruit bodies stick **straight out of the cone** — as if the cone had "sprouted mushrooms".

## The mushroom on a cone

The ecological headline: *Baeospora myosura* feeds **exclusively on conifer cones** — it decomposes half-rotted spruce and pine cones dropped the previous year. To find it, lift a cone and see a "mushroom forest" on every scale. It is one of the narrowest ecological niches: the "cone specialist" among fungi.

## Edibility

Edible — the flesh is thin but safe; gathering is impractical (nail-sized), though guides do mark it "edible small". Its value is in the starring role of "mushroom on a cone": a find for the photo and a lesson in ecology.

## Ecology

A cone saprotroph: spruce, pine, rarely other conifers; half-rotted cones from last year's fall. In colonies — 5–30 fruit bodies on one cone. Summer–autumn, most active after rain.

## Lookalikes

- **Strobilurus esculentus**: larger, on pine cones, a stem with a "wheel".
- **Strobilurus tenacellus**: pine cones, a long stem, a white "collar".
- **Mycena seynii**: on spruce cones, but with a radish smell and a "collar" under the cap.
- **Small mycenas on needles**: on litter, not on a cone.
