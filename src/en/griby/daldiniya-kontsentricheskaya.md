## Description

Fruit bodies — ball-shaped "lumps" 2–7 cm sitting directly on bark; outside a hard black shiny crust as if charred. In section — the signature trait: **concentric silvery-grey and dark rings**, like a layer cake (hence *concentrica*). Old specimens are dry and ring when tapped. Mature surfaces carry a reddish-brown spore dust that stains the hands.

## The cakes of King Alfred

The English name comes from the legend of King Alfred burning the cakes. The second name — "coal fungus" — is literal: dry old fruit bodies **ignite from a spark like charcoal** and smoulder, holding heat — they were used to carry fire and as kindling. In camp craft it is one of the best natural fire-steels.

## Year-round on ash

An ash specialist (less often beech, birch, oak): the fungus colonises the dying tree and erupts in "coals" on the bark — often by the dozen. Their hardness survives winter: the fruit bodies hang **all year** without rotting — a rare trait among fungi.

## Edibility

Inedible: a hard woody mass that cannot be chewed, with no culinary value. The worth is utilitarian (tinder) and ecological (decomposer of dead wood).

## Lookalikes

- **Other cramp-ball genera** (*Hypoxylon*, *Annulohypoxylon*): smaller "cushions" without clear concentric rings in section.
- **Young Pisolithus**: those grow from the ground and are yellowish-brown.
- **Bulgaria inquinans**: black rubbery "cups", not hard balls.

## Ecology

A saprotroph and weak parasite of dying hardwoods. The "coal balls" are among the most recognisable forest fungi: black dots on grey ash bark visible from tens of metres away.
