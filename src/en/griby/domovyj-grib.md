## What it is

Not a foraging mushroom but a **structural pest**: a basidiomycete that decomposes timber inside houses. Rare in nature (Asian mountain spruce forests), yet centuries of domestication made it Europe's principal "indoor" wood destroyer.

## How to recognise it

- **Mycelium**: white fan-like sheets and grey "cords" (rhizomorphs) up to 1 cm thick — the fungus crawls through brickwork and plaster toward new timber.
- **Fruit bodies**: folded, plate-like rusty-orange "puddles" on joists and floorboards with a white margin; they exude water droplets — hence *lacrymans*.
- **Timber**: cracks into cubes (brown rot), sounds hollow, crumbles.

## Biology

The genome was sequenced in 2011 (Science, PMID 21764756): *S. lacrymans* carries a rich cellulose- and lignin-degrading toolkit but has shed part of its "forest" gene set — it evolutionarily specialised on worked timber in dry interiors, a niche that barely exists in the wild.

## Control

Dry rot cannot be stopped by antiseptic alone: remove the moisture source (leaks, poor ventilation), cut out affected timber with a margin, dry and treat the structure. Rhizomorphs can "jump" metres of masonry — the fungus may live in a house for years before breaking out through the wallpaper.

## Practice

White fans, grey cords or rusty fruiting patches on joists, boards or in the basement mean calling a timber-decay specialist: this is not "mould" but a fungus capable of ruining floors within years.
