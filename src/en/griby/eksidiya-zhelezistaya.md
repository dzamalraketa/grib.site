## Description

Fruit bodies — black jelly masses 1–3 cm: separate "drops" with a shiny surface and characteristic **warty bumps** (hence *glandulosa*). They fuse into solid black "smears" up to 20 cm. In dry weather — hard "coal lumps" on the bark; in rain — they soften into slippery "butter". Inside — dark jelly.

## "Black butter" — sibling of the yellow brain

Same jelly-fungus group as our *Tremella mesenterica* (yellow brain): both are jellies that dry into a hard crust and "revive" in rain. But Exidia is **black** and grows on hardwoods (oak, beech), while Tremella is yellow and parasitises the crust fungus Peniophora. Exidia is a simple wood saprotroph.

## "Witches' butter" versus "witches' butter"

In English it is *black witches' butter*; European names — "Hexenbutter" and "warzenbutter" (wart butter). The "butter" refers to the slippery consistency in rain: a softened Exidia smears like black butter across the bark.

## Edibility

Inedible: a jelly mass with no bulk or flavour. Not toxic — in some cuisines similar jelly fungi go into soups (like our Auricularia), but Exidia is too small and tasteless.

## Lookalikes

- **Exidia nigricans** (strict witches' butter): glossier, fusing into bigger masses — hard to separate without microscopy.
- **Bulgaria inquinans**: black "cups", not warty "lumps".
- **Daldinia** (cramp balls): hard and round, not jelly — a different structure entirely.

## Ecology

A saprotroph of dead hardwood — branches, stumps, fallen trunks of oak and beech; less often other hardwoods. One of the "winter" fungi: the surviving "coal lump" hangs on bark year-round, spreading into "butter" during thaws.
