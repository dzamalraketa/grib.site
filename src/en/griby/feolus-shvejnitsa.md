## Description

Fruit body — a large velvety conk 10–30 cm, often several fused fans on a shared stalk; the upper surface is **downy like suede**, colour yellow-green → orange-brown → dark brown with age. The underside is not pores but a **winding "maze" of gills** (hence "mazegill"): yellow-green, **instantly browning** when pressed. The stalk is short, thick and runs into the soil — to the tree's roots. Flesh yellowish-brown, soft when young then corky.

## A dye that kills the tree

The fungus yields **saturated golden-green and yellow-brown dyes** — "dyer's mazegill" in the wool-dyers' trade: without a metal mordant it gives a remarkable green-gold rare among natural dyes. Yet it is also a serious **forest pest**: it causes red butt rot of roots and stem base in pines, hollowing out old trees.

## "A parasite growing out of the ground"

Unlike other brackets, Phaeolus most often fruits **not on the trunk but from the ground** at the tree's base — the mycelium reaches the roots beneath the soil, and the conk pops up like a "stalked cap". It is therefore mistaken for an ordinary gilled mushroom — but the "maze-gilled" underside and its site (strictly at a conifer) give it away at once.

## Edibility

Inedible: bitter, corky flesh, plus a forest parasite not worth gathering. Its value is dyeing and diagnostic: a conk at a pine's base is a signal of **root and butt rot** in the tree.

## Ecology

A parasite of roots and butt on living conifers (pine, spruce, larch), then a saprotroph on the killed host. It causes **butt rot** — a red heart-rot of the stem base that weakens old trees to the point of windthrow. One of the most serious diseases of mature conifer stands.
