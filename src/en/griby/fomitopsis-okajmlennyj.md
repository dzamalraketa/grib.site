## Description

Conks 5–30 cm, hoof- or shelf-shaped, sessile, cork-hard. The surface is varnished, with a characteristic **red-orange to red-brown "belt"** at the growing margin (hence "red-belted"); central zones grey to nearly black. The underside is a white to cream pore surface that browns when bruised. The margin is whitish, sometimes weeping droplets — hence the folk name "weeping conk".

## The forest's perennial calendar

Each year the conk adds **one growth ring** — like a tree. Old specimens with a dozen zones live **for decades**, outliving their host and the forager who first spotted them. It is among the most abundant brackets on Earth: found on any dead timber from taiga to city parks.

## A decomposer, not a killer

*F. pinicola* is a typical **dead-wood** decay fungus (brown rot, see `lignin-enzymes-review-2010`): it colonises logs, stumps and downed trunks. On a living tree the conk appears only through a wound — the fungus does not kill, it moves in after. In sawmills it rots stacked timber — hence the economic damage.

## Edibility

Inedible: cork-hard flesh that cannot be chewed. Not toxic — in Siberia it was brewed as "poor man's chaga", but there is no proven benefit. Status: `inedible`.

## Lookalikes

- **Fomes fomentarius** (tinder fungus): greyer, hoof-shaped, **no red belt** — the key distinction.
- **Phellinus** spp.: brown, without the lacquered surface.
- **Ganoderma**: varnished, stalked — a different habit.

## Ecology

A saprotroph of all dead species — pine to birch; a perennial conk that acts as the forest's "old-timer". One of the first fungi spotted in winter: the white underside and red belt show through bare woodland.
