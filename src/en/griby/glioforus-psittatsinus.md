## Description

Cap 2–5 cm, bell-shaped → flattened; when young a **saturated emerald green** with a slimy surface, then fading through yellow-green to ochre-yellow or even pinkish. Gills adnate, widely spaced, first greenish then yellow — with the "waxy" thickness and sparseness of the group. Stem 3–8 cm, slender, slimy, greenish → yellow. The whole fungus is very **slippery** — slime like the porcelain fungus. Spore print white.

## Green, but neither poisonous nor edible

The only "emerald" among waxcaps: the green is a cuticle pigment, not a warning. Not toxic — but the flesh is thin, slimy and tasteless; status `inedible` for quality, not danger. Tiny and fragile, *G. psittacinus* is a subject for the camera, not the pot.

## Green → yellow chameleon

The emerald cap lasts only days: the fungus then "ripens" into yellow and orange. On one meadow you may see green, yellow-green and orange fruit bodies at once — and beginners count them as three species. The test is **slime**: if cap and stem are coated in sticky mucus, it is *psittacinus* whatever its colour.

## Sibling of the witch's hat

From the same ecological guild as our *Hygrocybe conica* (witch's hat): both are **indicators of old unfertilised grassland**, where waxcaps form whole communities. A parrot waxcap on a meadow signals soil untouched by nitrogen fertiliser for decades.

## Ecology

A saprotroph (or possibly biotroph — waxcap ecology is still debated) on old meadows, pastures and heaths. Fragile: it vanishes when a meadow is fertilised or ploughed. In Russia — the western and central belt, uncommon.
