## Description

Fruit body 5–15 cm, fan- or funnel-shaped, often fused into clustered "bouquets". Upper surface violet-lilac → greyish brown, browning with age. The key feature is the **underside**: not gills but a **wrinkled-folded**, almost smooth violet surface with wave-like ridges — like the inside of an ear (hence "pig's ears"). Flesh white, thick at the base, odourless. Spore print ochre.

## Why "pig's ears", not a chanterelle

Gomphus sits in the same clade as chanterelles, but the differences are instant: it is **violet** and **folded, not veined**. Chanterelles carry dense forked ridges; Gomphus shows a continuous wrinkled membrane. So close that it was once *Cantharellus clavatus* — but molecular work placed it in *Gomphus/Turbinellus*.

## Red-listed status

In many European countries it appears in regional Red Data Books: the mushroom is tied to **old-growth conifer stands** with undisturbed litter and disappears with them. In the Caucasus and Carpathians it persists in surviving spruce forest. Our status — `conditional`: it is technically edible (the base flesh is edible after cooking, though often bitter), but more importantly **picking it is unethical**: every harvested fruit body is a blow to a shrinking population.

## In the kitchen (where legal)

Where collection is permitted: the basal flesh is firm, comparable to a chanterelle, but needs blanching and often carries a bitter edge. Young "ears" are pickled. The violet upper zone is tough — it is trimmed away.

## Lookalikes

- **Turbinellus floccosus** (scaly chanterelle): orange-brown and scaly, inedible — different colour and texture.
- **Chanterelles**: yellow, ridge-veined, never in fused bouquets.
- **False chanterelle**: orange, with true thin gills.
