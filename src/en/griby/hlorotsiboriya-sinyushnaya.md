## Description

Cups 2–6 mm, miniature, on a short stalk; colour **turquoise-green to blue-green**, the outer surface paler. In dry weather the cups shrivel and fade; in rain they are vivid again. They grow in groups on bare (bark-free) rotting wood. Spores white, small — under the microscope recognisable by their narrow thread-like septa.

## Tiffany-coloured timber

The elfcup's main phenomenon is not the cups but the **mycelial dye**: the mycelium produces the pigment **xylindein**, saturating the wood in an unreal **turquoise-green**. Pieces of "green timber" appear on rotting branches and boards — the fungus's signature even when no cups are present. Such stained wood has been prized for centuries in **marquetry** — wood inlay: "green oak" went into the Tunbridge ware tradition.

## Two near-identical sisters

*C. aeruginascens* and *C. aeruginosa* are twins distinguishable only by spore size under the microscope. Most field finds are assigned to *aeruginascens*; for "green wood" recording the distinction is moot — both produce xylindein.

## Edibility

Inedible: microscopic cups with no bulk or flavour. The value is aesthetic and craft-based (stained wood for inlay) and ecological: a white-rot decomposer of lignin.

## Ecology

A saprotroph of rotting hardwood — oak, beech, birch. The fungus itself is scarce in cup form — more often you see its **trace**: turquoise stains and bands on rotten logs, the signature of hidden mycelium.
