## Description

Annual brackets 8–25 cm, semicircular or cushion-like, **soft and watery** when young. Upper side: **bright rusty-orange, coarsely hairy ("shaggy")** — hence the name. Underside: a rusty-brown tube layer with angular pores. Young fruit bodies exude **rusty droplet "tears"** on the lower surface — the "weeping" trait. By autumn it hardens, blackens, and by winter crumbles; last year's charred-black remnants often hang on the tree for another year.

## The "weeping" parasite

The wet rusty droplets on the underside are a signature detail: a young inonotus literally "sweats" rusty liquid. Against pale ash bark, a rusty shaggy mass studded with droplets is one of summer's most conspicuous fungi.

## Ecology: heart rot

*Inonotus hispidus* is a **parasite of living trees** causing central (heart) rot: the mycelium consumes the trunk's core — the tree stands green outside while hollow inside. Typical hosts are **ash** (often in parks and along roads), oak, apple, walnut, elm. Finding a bracket is a diagnosis: the tree is weakened, and the windthrow risk has grown.

## Edibility

Inedible — soft, watery, bitter, worthless; there are folk mentions of brewing it into tea, but with no evidential base.

## Lookalikes

- **Dyer's mazegill**: a labyrinthine underside, at the base of pines.
- **Chicken of the woods**: yellow-orange overlapping shelves on oak, with finer pores.
- **Young Phellinus**: not so rusty and not "shaggy", and perennial.
