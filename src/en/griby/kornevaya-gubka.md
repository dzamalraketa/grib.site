## Description

Fruiting bodies are small brackets or "collars" 2–10 cm at the **base of a trunk or on roots**, often half-hidden under moss and litter. Upper side: brown, concentrically grooved. The underside is the key: **a creamy-white tube layer with small round pores** (the "sponge"!). Actively fruiting brackets weep droplets. On stumps they show as flat whitish "pancakes" at the root collar. The smell is mushroomy, faintly resinous.

## Pathogen number one

*Heterobasidion annosum* is not "just a mushroom" but **the most destructive forest pathogen of the temperate zone**: it causes annosum root and stem rot, killing spruce, pine, fir and larch by the millions of cubic metres of timber a year. It infects through **fresh stumps** at felling: spores land on the cut, the mycelium invades the stump roots and crosses via root grafts into the roots of neighbouring living trees. So the disease "walks" through the forest from stump to stump.

## Why a forager should know it

A root-rot bracket at the foot of a spruce means the tree is **doomed**: its root core is rotten, the trunk stands on bark alone, and the windthrow risk is up. For a forester it is the signal to treat stumps (urea, biofungicides). For a forager — "look, don't pick nearby": an infected patch is the forest's "sick zone".

## Edibility

Inedible — bitter tubed flesh; the economic interest is purely as an enemy of the forest.

## Ecology

A parasite: fresh stumps and roots of conifers (spruce the chief victim), rarely broadleaf. White collars at the root collar are the fruiting stage; the real "work" is mycelium in the roots.

## Lookalikes

- **Tinder conk**: on beech, perennial conks, not at the root collar.
- **Artist's conk**: lacquered top, brown pores — the sponge is white.
- **Red-belted conk**: a red band, heart-rot too, but not "root" rot.
- **Young phellinuses**: hooves on trunks, not at ground level.
