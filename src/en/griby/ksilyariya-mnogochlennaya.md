## Description

Fruit bodies — **knobbly "fingers"** 3–8 cm tall, tufted from one point on a stump; in spring the young ones are grey-white with pale tips (a dusting of conidia), by summer and autumn **coal-black**, hard, with a matt "fossilised" surface. Inside — white woody flesh with black spore chambers.

## "Fingers" — and why they look so grim

A cluster of black "fingers" sticking out of a half-rotted stump is one of the most Halloween-worthy sights in mycology. The reality is humbler: these are perennial stromata — each "finger" lives and fruits for years.

## Ecology — the stump's undertaker

A saprotroph on broadleaf wood (beech, oak, maple, rarely elm) — it breaks down lignin and cellulose in old stumps. Sometimes a weak parasite on the weakened roots of living trees. One of the forest's "undertakers", without which stumps would never rot away on time.

## Practice

Inedible — a woody mass. But finding dead man's fingers is a mandatory item on the spring–autumn foraging quest: they photograph wonderfully and are entirely harmless.
