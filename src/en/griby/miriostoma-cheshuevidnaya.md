## Description

Fruit body — a ball-shaped "box" 2–5 cm sitting on several short "stalks" (mycelial cords); the outer skin splits into 5–12 "rays" that bend back, lifting the ball — an earthstar on stalks. The inner spore sac is **greyish-brown** with characteristic **bumps** — but the key trait: **dozens of tiny pores** across the whole surface through which spores escape — the "pepper pot".

## A "salt shaker" for spores — a sieve, not a neck

In ordinary earthstars (*Geastrum*) spores exit through **one** central hole at the top of the sac. In Myriostoma there are **hundreds of micropores** across the surface, like a salt shaker: spores "sift out" with every gust of wind or raindrop. A unique design: the only fungus with a "sieve" for spores instead of a "neck".

## A red-listed rarity

*M. coliforme* is one of the **rarest** earth fungi of the northern hemisphere: in most European countries it is red-listed, in Russia — in regional registers (Caucasus, the south). It appears sporadically on sandy soils and vanishes for years: "found a Myriostoma" is an event on the level of "met a wildcat".

## Edibility

Inedible — microscopic size and corky body; its value is purely as a "trophy" for the photographer.

## Lookalikes

- **Geastrum** spp. (ordinary earthstars): one central hole, not a "sieve".
- **Astraeus hygrometricus** (our hygroscopic earthstar): opens in rain, without multiple pores.
- **Sphaerobolus**: a microscopic "cannon" — an entirely different scale.

## Ecology

A saprotroph of sandy, calcareous soils — conifer edges, dry meadows, sometimes sandy gardens. The "sieve" is presumably an adaptation to sparse spores: the more openings, the higher the chance that at least one fires in the wind.
