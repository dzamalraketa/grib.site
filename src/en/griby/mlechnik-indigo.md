## Description

Cap 5–15 cm, depressed → funnel-shaped, margin initially enrolled; colour a **saturated indigo blue**, fading to silvery-grey with age, often with concentric zones. Gills adnate, the same blue, **greening** when bruised. Stem 2–6 cm, short, blue, sometimes pitted. Flesh brittle — and on cutting it exudes **blue milky latex** that slowly turns green in the air. It is the only mushroom on Earth with such latex.

## The only blue "bleeder"

The pigment is an **azulene derivative** (1-stearoyloxymethylene-7-isopropenyl-4-methylazulene), related to compounds in chamomile and wormwood. Wounded tissue does not fade — the latex oxidises to green, the same effect seen on the gills. The blue survives even cooking: in Mexico indigo is sold in markets as "azul", keeping its colour in tacos and soups.

## Edibility and flavour

Edible — but a "star of the plate" for colour, not taste: the texture is coarse-grained, the flavour bitter-peppery, and many guides call it "mediocre". In Mexico, Guatemala and China it is a traditional harvest. The blue latex dyes the whole pot, so indigo is usually fried on its own.

## Lookalikes

- **Lactarius chelidonium** and *L. paradoxus*: bluish milk caps of the Pacific region, but their latex is yellow or red-tinted — never indigo.
- **Any other milk cap**: none exudes blue latex — an unambiguous character of the species.

## Why it is in the catalogue

*L. indigo* demonstrates that colour in mushrooms is chemistry, not "warning". We are trained that bright = poisonous. Indigo breaks the rule: the saturated blue here is an edible azulene pigment, not a defence. Placed next to scarlet Sarcoscypha and Clathrus, it completes the palette of "safe brightness".
