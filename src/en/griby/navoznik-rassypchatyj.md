## Description

Cap 0.5–1.5 cm, egg-shaped → bell-shaped, **pale grey to creamy-buff**, with fine radial grooves — nearly translucent, "porcelain". Gills free, white → grey-brown (they do **not** ink!). Stem thread-like (1–3 cm), white, brittle. They grow in **huge dense colonies** — hundreds and thousands of bells covering a stump, log or the ground at a root in one solid "fungal rug".

## "Fairy bonnets"

This is the most colonial of the inkcaps: not a tuft and not a row but an **army**. A single old oak or willow stump can break out in thousands of tiny parasols — hence the English "fairy bonnet". In the morning they stand in even ranks — by evening they "melt" (small inkcaps are short-lived).

## It does not ink!

Unlike the "inky" inkcaps ([shaggy](/en/griby/navoznik-belyj/), grey, glistening), disseminatus **does not deliquesce into ink** with age — the caps simply dry and drop. A key to ID: "inkcap on a stump in the hundreds, not blackening" is almost certainly this one.

## Edibility

Technically **edible** (non-inking, non-toxic), but nail-sized: collecting a thousand bonnets for a frying pan makes no sense. "Edible" here is an honest "not harmful" rather than an invitation.

## Ecology

A saprotroph of broadleaf wood and buried organics — old stumps, logs, buried roots, compost heaps. It "follows" the larger decomposers: it arrives on an already half-digested substrate and finishes the leftovers. May–October, especially after rain.

## Lookalikes

- **Other small coprinelluses** (*C. hiascens*, *C. pellucidus*): even smaller, on soil/litter; disseminatus is on wood in thousands.
- **Glistening inkcap**: shiny scales, inks.
- **Mycenas**: on single logs, not in "carpets" and not so pale-grey.
- **Psathyrella**: browner, more fragile gills, not in such "armies".
