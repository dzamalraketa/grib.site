## Description

Cap 5–15 cm, convex → flat; a cream-white ground covered in **large brown scales**, thinning toward the margin — "snakeskin". Gills **serrated** (toothed edges — a hallmark of the genus), white → cream, slightly decurrent. Stem 3–8 cm, stout, scaly below a cuff-like ring (often rubbed off). Flesh white, firm, smelling faintly of aniseed or just "mushroomy". Spore print white.

## "Train wrecker" — literally

The English name is no metaphor: *N. lepideus* is one of the few fungi able to rot wood impregnated with **creosote** — a ferocious antiseptic. Its mycelium riddled railway sleepers, telegraph poles and mine props until they crumbled; hence real derailments and the name. It causes a **brown rot**: cellulose is digested, leaving a brown lignin "skeleton".

## Why inedible

The flesh is not poisonous, but old specimens are cork-hard, young ones bitter; moreover it is gathered mainly on industrial timber where it accumulates preservative chemicals. Status: `inedible`.

## Ecology and resilience

A saprotroph-decomposer of dead conifer wood. Unique in its tolerance: it grows on **creosote, copper sulphate and dry beams**, surviving where other fungi fail. In nature — pine and spruce stumps and fallen trunks; the anthropogenic niche — all wooden building heritage.

## Lookalikes

- **Pholiota squarrosa**: brown print, yellow flesh, clusters on living trees.
- **Golden pholiota**: golden scales, dark below the ring — a different species.
- **Honey fungi**: smaller, no serrated gills, brown print.
