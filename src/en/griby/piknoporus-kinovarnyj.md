## Description

Shelves 3–12 cm, semicircular, thick corky-woody (5–15 mm). The entire surface is **cinnabar-red to orange-scarlet** (the very colour of the mineral cinnabar — hence the name): the top is felty-velvety, zoned, glossy; the underside is a tube layer of the same bright red, with fine pores. Flesh tough, corky, **also red** — cut it open and the inside is the same scarlet tone. In old conks the colour dulls to orange-pink.

## Cinnabar on a log

*Pycnoporus cinnabarinus* is one of the brightest fungi of the temperate forest: a red "brick" on dead birch or willow is visible from tens of metres off in winter, like a flag. The whole mushroom is painted by one pigment — outside, inside, and in the pores. That pigment (trametrubin and relatives) belongs to the same chemical family as the chlorociboria turquoise.

## Ecology

A saprotroph — **white rot** on dead broadleaf wood: birch, beech, willow, alder, rowan. Logs, stumps, worked timber. Year-round — conks are perennial, old ones persist and fade.

## Edibility

Inedible — tough corky flesh; the red colour is a "look, don't eat" signal. Its value is **visual**: the most conspicuous winter bracket for observation.

## Lookalikes

- **Pycnoporus sanguineus** (*P. coccineus*): even redder, denser pores — subtropical; rarer here.
- **Pycnoporellus fulgens**: more orange, a labyrinthine pore surface.
- **Turkey tail**: zoned, but not uniformly red.
- **Orange peel / jelly fungi**: gelatinous, not corky.
