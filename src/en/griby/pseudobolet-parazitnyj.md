## Description

Cap 2–6 cm, convex then flat, thin-fleshed; colour greyish-yellow to dull brown, sometimes olive-tinted. Pore surface yellow then olive, pores large and angular; barely bruising blue (or weakly so). Stem 3–8 cm, often curved, club-shaped at the base — which is **embedded in the host's body**. Flesh pale yellow, without a distinct smell.

## A mushroom living on a mushroom

*P. parasiticus* is the rarest trick among boletes: an obligate **mycoparasite**. It erupts through the fruit body of *Scleroderma citrinum* (the common earthball — it has its own card in our catalogue), feeding on its tissues. Sometimes a whole cluster of yellow boletes sprouts from a single potato-like ball — a sight unlike anything else in the forest.

"Parasite or saprotroph?" was long debated: microscopy showed the pseudoboletus consumes the scleroderma's actual tissue rather than merely using the ball as substrate. This is the second tier of parasitism in our catalogue — after the slime mould's host and Tremella's Peniophora.

## How to find it

Do not look for the bolete — look for **earthballs**: yellow-brown leathery-skinned balls under pine and spruce on acid soil. If a yellow bolete is growing out of a ball, it is *P. parasiticus* — there is no alternative. Appearances are erratic: in an "earthball year" there may be many, then none for seasons.

## Edibility

Inedible — not toxic, but the flesh is thin, tasteless and bitter. Its value is purely spectacular: seeing a bolete erupting from an earthball is a rare field find.

## Lookalikes

- **Young yellow boletes** (xerocomelli, suilli): grow **from the ground**, not from a ball.
- **Gyroporus castaneus**: hollow stem and an earthen base — a five-second distinction.
