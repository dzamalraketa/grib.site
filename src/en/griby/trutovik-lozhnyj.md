## Description

Perennial conks, **hoof-shaped or hemispherical** (5–20 cm), very hard and woody. Upper side: first rusty-brown and felty, soon **blackening and cracking** into a "charred" crust with concentric grooves — hence igniarius ("of fire"). The margin is ochre-rusty. Underside: a brown tube layer with fine pores. Each year the conk adds a new tube layer — **the fungus can be "dated" by its layers**, like tree rings.

## Amadou: fungus tinder

The historical role of *Phellinus igniarius* is **amadou (tinder fungus)**: the inner layer of a young conk was boiled, dried and beaten into a soft "leather" that smouldered from a spark and held the ember for hours. Travellers and nomads carried a piece of amadou in a tinderbox — "portable fire" before matches. To the resourceful forest, it is a prehistoric lighter.

## The "hoof" — a sign the tree is ill

*Phellinus igniarius* is a **parasite of living trees** causing white heart-rot of the trunk. A hoof-conk on a living willow or birch marks a weakened tree: the core is hollow, the trunk held together by bark. Old willows with rows of black "hooves" are a fungal "verdict" hanging over the water.

## Edibility

Inedible — stone-hard woody flesh. Folk phellinus teas are recorded but have no evidential base.

## Ecology

A saprotroph-parasite: willow, birch, aspen, bird cherry, less often other broadleaf hosts. Conks live **for years and decades** — an old "hoof" may outlive its host tree.

## Lookalikes

- **Artist's conk / tinder conk** (*Fomes fomentarius*): on beech/birch; its top does not blacken and crack so early; same amadou use.
- **Phellinus tremulae**: on aspen, smaller and softer.
- **Shaggy bracket**: annual, soft, rusty, "weeps" — not a perennial hoof.
- **Young ganodermas**: lacquered surface when mature.
