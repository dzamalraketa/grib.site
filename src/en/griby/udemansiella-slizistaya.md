## Description

Cap 2–8 cm, hemispherical → flat; colour grey-white to ivory, **translucent**, coated in a thick layer of slime — hence the porcelain sheen. Gills broad, **widely spaced**, white, sinuate-adnate, with veining between them. Stem 3–10 cm, slender, often curved, with a **thin ring**; below it greyish-scaly. Spore print white with **very large** spores (to 18 µm — visible to the naked eye on black paper).

## A porcelain chandelier in the beech

The fungus is a weak parasite of living beech and colonises dead wood: fruit bodies erupt on bare branches 5–15 m above ground, where they are backlit by the sky — hence its "unreachable porcelain" reputation. The cap slime is protection against drying in the exposed canopy; in rain the caps are so slippery they will not stay in the hand.

## Edibility and harvest

Edible and even tasty — young, still-opaque caps are tender with a slight sweetness. The challenge is logistics: gathering "porcelain" takes a hooked pole or a shaken branch. The practical option is collecting from windthrown boughs — the fungus fruits on fallen wood too. The slime rinses off under running water, leaving clean white flesh.

## Lookalikes

- **Mycena and Marasmius** on beech twigs: smaller, without ring or slimy sheen.
- **Pluteus** on stumps: pink spore print, no slime and no ring.
- **Rooting shank** (*O. radicata*): a browner cap and a long rooting stem "tail" — a different build.

## Ecology and relict status

A beech specialist — in primeval beech forest of the Carpathians and Europe it forms "porcelain tiers" in the canopy. An indicator of old broadleaf woodland; scarce in young plantations. The slime doubles as antibacterial defence: the fungus lives at a height where competition for substrate is fierce.
