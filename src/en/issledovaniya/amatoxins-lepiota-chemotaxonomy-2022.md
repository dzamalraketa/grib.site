Sgambelluri R.M. et al. Occurrence and chemotaxonomical analysis of amatoxins in Lepiota spp. (Agaricales). Phytochemistry. 2022;195:113060.

## Why this study

About **95% of fatal mushroom poisonings** are caused by amatoxins — and the genus *Lepiota* gets them attributed to dozens of species by default. Until this paper nobody had broadly tested **which** lepiotas actually contain them.

## Methods

- **48 freshly collected specimens of 16 Lepiota species**.
- UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS with chromatography and mass fragmentation; benchmark — *Amanita phalloides*.
- Phylogenetic analysis — to see whether the toxic species form one clade.

## Findings

- **Amatoxins detected** in: *L. subincarnata* (α- and γ-amanitin), *L. brunneoincarnata* (α-amanitin + amaninamide), *L. elaiophylla* (α- and β-amanitin).
- **Possible new amatoxin derivatives** — in *L. boudieri* and *L. elaiophylla*.
- **12 species clean** — including the common *L. cristata*, *L. clypeolaria*, *L. castanea*, *L. lilacea*.
- The toxic species form a **monophyletic group** — i.e. amatoxin in lepiotas is evolutionarily concentrated, not spread across the genus.

## Why it matters

The same approach as Mycologia-2023 for gyromitrin: "genus = toxin" is a convenient but inaccurate formula. The paper refines our `lepiota-burorozovaya` card: the danger is specifically it and its closest relatives — not "any little parasol". The practical rule stands — small lepiotas are never eaten — but now we know exactly which ones are proven to carry amatoxin.
