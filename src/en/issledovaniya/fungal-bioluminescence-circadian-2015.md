Curr Biol 2015. Circadian control sheds light on fungal bioluminescence (PMID 25802150).

## Why this study

Our bitter oysterling card mentions the North American "foxfire": a faint green glow. But why should a fungus glow at all? This work shows it is **not a random byproduct** but a biologically regulated function.

## What was measured

- Mycelial glow of *Neonothopanus gardneri* in field and lab conditions.
- Temperature compensation — the mark of a true circadian clock.
- Attraction of nocturnal insects (potential spore dispersers).

## Findings

- The glow is **under circadian control**: it peaks **at night**, and the rhythm persists at constant temperature (temperature compensation — the classic signature of a biological clock).
- This rejects the "random byproduct of oxidative metabolism" hypothesis: the glow is **deliberately** confined to nighttime.
- The proposed function: **attracting nocturnal insects** that carry spores — light as a "beacon" for dispersers.

## Why it matters

- ~71 glowing fungal species — and all emit green light (λ≈530 nm): the same signal in different evolutionary lines — convergent adaptation.
- The glow is not "light for light's sake" but **communication**: the fungus switches on its beacon exactly when its carriers are active.
- For our Panellus card: the American glow is an adaptation, the European "absence" is a different strain strategy — not a "broken lamp".
