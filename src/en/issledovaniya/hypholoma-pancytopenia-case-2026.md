Medicine (Baltimore) 2026. Delayed pancytopenia following ingestion of wild mushrooms consistent with *Hypholoma fasciculare*: a case report (PMID 42994845).

## Why this study

Our new [sulphur tuft card](/en/griby/lozhnoopyonok-sernozhyoltyj/) describes the classic gastrointestinal syndrome. This case shows a **second, delayed face of toxicity**: bone-marrow suppression days after the meal — with no dramatic start.

## What happened

- **Patient**: a 57-year-old man who ate self-collected wild mushrooms.
- **Latency ~10 days**: fever and generalized weakness — the classic trap of delayed syndromes: the link to the mushroom is easily missed.
- **Work-up**: pancytopenia; infectious, autoimmune and hematologic causes (including bone-marrow examination) excluded — leaving toxin-mediated marrow suppression as the most plausible diagnosis.
- **Treatment**: conservative monitoring and supportive care; no specific antidote exists.
- **Outcome**: blood parameters gradually recovered completely, with no recurrence on follow-up.

## Why it matters

- **Latency is the key**: mushroom poisoning can be delayed — 10 quiet days do not mean "all clear". Keeping the fact of eating wild mushrooms in mind matters for patient and clinician alike.
- **A hematologic form is rare**: this widens the picture of *Hypholoma* beyond "just diarrhoea"; for clinicians — a signal to check blood counts even after a mild start.
- **The practical take-away** matches our card: sulphur tuft is not a "bitter but harmless" honey-fungus double, but a species with potentially severe delayed consequences.

## Limitations

- **A single case**, n=1 — frequency and dose-dependence cannot be estimated.
- Mushroom identification is "consistent with" the description/context, not molecular confirmation of the meal's remains.
- The diagnosis rests on excluding alternatives — strict causality is only partly proven.
