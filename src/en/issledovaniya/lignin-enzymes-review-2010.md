J Basic Microbiol 2010. Lignin-modifying enzymes in filamentous basidiomycetes — ecological, functional and phylogenetic review (PMID 20175122).

## Why this study

Our new decomposer cards — Neolentinus lepideus ("train wrecker"), Daldinia concentrica, dry rot — all lead to the same question: **how does a fungus digest wood**? This review systematises the enzymatic arsenal.

## What the review systematises

- Wood-decay fungi are unique: they are the only organisms able to degrade **lignin** — the aromatic polymer that makes wood tough.
- **White rot** (most polypores): secretion of lignin peroxidases, Mn-peroxidases and **laccases** — the fungus takes both lignin and cellulose, bleaching the wood.
- **Brown rot** (our Neolentinus, dry rot): a reduced enzyme set — the fungus digests **cellulose only**, leaving a brown lignin "skeleton" — hence the name.
- Phylogenetic analysis shows white rot is the **ancestral** state; brown rot evolved independently in several lineages as an "economy strategy".

## Why it matters

- These enzymes drive the **planetary carbon cycle**: without fungi, wood would never return to the soil.
- A practical lesson for our Neolentinus card: its ability to live on **creosote** is an extreme case of the same enzymatic power over complex organic molecules.
- The white/brown rot distinction defines a fungus's **role** in the forest: white rot returns everything, brown rot leaves lignin — and builds humus.
