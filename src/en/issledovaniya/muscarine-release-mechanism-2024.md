Angew Chem Int Ed Engl. 2024. The Fatal Mushroom Neurotoxin Muscarine is Released from a Harmless Phosphorylated Precursor upon Cellular Injury (PMID 39432715).

## Why this study

Our **fool's funnel** card describes the muscarinic syndrome: sweating, tearing, bradycardia. But a question remained: how does a mushroom hold a potent neurotoxin inside its own tissues without poisoning itself? This paper gives an elegant answer — and explains why muscarine acts precisely after ingestion.

## Findings

- In *Clitocybe rivulosa* mycelium the major natural product is **4′-phosphomuscarine**, a previously overlooked phosphorylated compound (structure confirmed by HPLC-MS and NMR plus a synthesized reference).
- The same precursor was detected in the fiber caps *Pseudosperma spectrale* and *Inocybe nitidiuscula* — the mechanism is broader than one species.
- At the M3 muscarinic receptor, phosphomuscarine shows **only weak affinity** — on its own it is nearly harmless.
- On **cellular injury** (chewing, digestion) the phosphate group is cleaved — and active L-(+)-muscarine is released.

## Why it matters

This is a "triggered" toxin: the mushroom stores an inert phosphorylated form and converts it into poison the moment tissue is destroyed — precisely when it is eaten. Practical implications:

- Drying and storage do not neutralise the danger: the precursor remains, and enzymatic release happens inside the gut.
- The muscarinic syndrome is not a surface effect but a biochemical trap keyed to tissue destruction.
- The described mechanism argues against "just a taste": even a small amount of chewed tissue starts the release.
