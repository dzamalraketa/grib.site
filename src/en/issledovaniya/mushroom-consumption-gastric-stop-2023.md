Ba D.M., Ssentongo P., Pelucchi C., Negri E., Palli D., …, Muscat J. Mushroom consumption and risk of gastric cancer: a pooled analysis within the stomach cancer pooling project and a combined meta-analysis with other observational studies. Eur J Cancer Prev. 2023;32(3):222-228.

## Rationale

Ba's earlier 2021 meta-analysis (our `mushroom-consumption-cancer-meta-2021` card) linked mushroom intake to lower overall cancer risk; here the same group does a targeted analysis on **individual-level data** for gastric cancer — stronger than a meta-analysis of published summary estimates.

## Methods

Pooled analysis of individual data from 11 **StoP (Stomach cancer Pooling)** studies: **3,900 gastric cancer cases** and **7,792 controls**. Mushroom intake by food-frequency questionnaires; adjusted for age, sex, education, smoking, alcohol and calories. Plus a combined meta-analysis with 4 previously published studies.

## Results

- High mushroom intake ↔ lower gastric cancer risk: **RR 0.82** (0.71–0.95) highest vs lowest category.
- Across the four earlier studies: RR 0.59 (0.26–1.33 — non-significant).
- Across all data: **RR 0.77** (0.66–0.91).
- The association held by sex and by histologic and anatomic subtype (cardia and non-cardia).

## Authors' conclusions

High mushroom consumption is associated with reduced gastric cancer risk in the largest pooled individual dataset to date.

## Worth noting

Individual-data pooled analysis is the strongest observational design available: unified variable recoding, standardised adjustments, less publication skew. But these are **still observational data**: mushroom consumption correlates with many other factors (lifestyle, income, overall diet), and causality is unproven. Mushrooms are not "protection" — they are one marker of a dietary pattern associated with lower risk.
