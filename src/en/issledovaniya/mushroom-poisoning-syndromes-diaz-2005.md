Diaz J.H. Evolving global epidemiology, syndromic classification, general management, and prevention of unknown mushroom poisonings. Crit Care Med. 2005;33(2):419-26.

## Rationale

Unknown-mushroom poisoning is the everyday scenario in toxicology: the clinician almost never knows the species. A classification **by presentation** — hours to symptoms and target organ — is what drives prognosis and management.

## Methods

Descriptive analysis of world literature: observational studies 1959–2002 and reports back to 1951 — **28,018 confirmed poisonings** (MEDLINE 1966–2004, OLDMEDLINE 1951–1965, library sources before 1951). Onset timing and organ target were extracted; secular fatality trends tested.

## Results

**14 syndromes grouped by onset:**

| Onset | Syndromes |
|---|---|
| Early (<6 h) | 8: four neurotoxic (muscarine, ibotenic acid/muscimol, psilocybin, coprine), two GI, two allergic |
| Late (6–24 h) | 3: hepatotoxic (amatoxins), accelerated nephrotoxicity, erythromelalgia |
| Delayed (≥1 day) | 3: delayed nephrotoxicity (orellanine), delayed neurotoxicity, rhabdomyolysis |

**Four newly formalised syndromes:**
- Accelerated nephrotoxicity — *Amanita proxima*, *A. smithiana* (kidneys fail within days, without hepatitis).
- Rhabdomyolysis — *Tricholoma equestre* (the man-on-horseback!), *Russula subnigricans*.
- Erythromelalgia — *Clitocybe amoenolens*, *C. acromelalgia*.
- Delayed neurotoxicity — *Hapalopilus rutilans*.

Overall trend: since the 1950s, reports of severe and fatal mushroom poisoning have risen worldwide.

## Author's conclusions

With an unknown mushroom, time-to-symptoms and target organ give the best working classification: early onset is usually benign; delayed onset warrants ICU and transplant readiness.

## Worth noting

Diaz's classification underpins modern toxicology protocols — and our own lookalike cards. Its weakness: a narrative review, not a meta-analysis — syndrome estimates are qualitative, and global case distribution is skewed by publication activity (Japan and Europe over-represent Africa). A separate lesson: *Tricholoma equestre* appears under "rhabdomyolysis" — which is exactly what our zelyonushka card warns about.
