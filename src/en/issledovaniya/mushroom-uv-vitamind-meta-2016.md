J Nutr 2016. Effect of Ultraviolet Light-Exposed Mushrooms on Vitamin D Status: LC-MS/MS Reanalysis of Biobanked Sera from an RCT and a Systematic Review plus Meta-Analysis (PMID 26865648).

## Rationale

The RCT data on UV mushrooms and 25(OH)D were "limited and mixed". The authors pooled all eligible RCTs and added their own LC-MS/MS reanalysis of sera — the gold standard for vitamin-D metabolites instead of the less accurate immunoassay.

## Methods

- Systematic search of Ovid MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL → **6 RCTs** of UV-exposed mushrooms reporting serum 25(OH)D.
- Reanalysis of biobanked sera from one RCT by **LC-MS/MS** for separate 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3.
- Meta-analysis using weighted mean difference (WMD).

## Results

- **Across all 6 RCTs**: 25(OH)D did not rise significantly (P=0.12), with high heterogeneity (I²=87%).
- **3 European RCTs** (baseline ~38.6 nmol/L — deficiency): **+15.2 nmol/L** gain, significant (P=0.03).
- **3 US RCTs** (baseline ~81.5 nmol/L — adequate): **no effect** (P=0.83).
- By vitamin form: UV mushrooms raise 25(OH)D2 but **lower 25(OH)D3** — displacement confirmed.

## Conclusions

UV-treated mushrooms improve vitamin D status **only where it was low** (European populations with deficiency); with adequate baseline there is no effect. The heterogeneity is explained precisely by differing baseline status and geography.

## Caveats

- The key framing for readers: "mushroom vitamin D" is a **tool against deficiency**, not a universal supplement. Expecting a boost where none is missing is futile.
- The D2→D3 displacement shows why **total** 25(OH)D is the right endpoint, not 25(OH)D2 alone.
- High heterogeneity (I²=87–88%) is an honest signal: real doses, mushroom species and UV protocols differed between trials; "any UV mushrooms" is too broad a category.
- The LC-MS/MS reanalysis showed immunoassays can distort separate D2/D3 accounting — a methodological detail that matters when reading older RCTs.
