Diaz J.H. Nephrotoxic Mushroom Poisoning: Global Epidemiology, Clinical Manifestations, and Management. Wilderness Environ Med. 2021;32(4):537-544.

## Rationale

In the public mind "deadly mushroom" = death cap → liver. But there is a second target — the **kidneys** — with a different onset, different species and different management.

## Methods

Narrative review of literature 1957–2021 on nephrotoxic mushroom poisonings and their management; classification by time to acute renal failure.

## What it describes

**Nephrotoxic groups:**
- **Orellanine webcaps** (*C. orellanus*, *C. rubellus* et al.) — delayed nephrotoxicity: symptoms after 3–14 days, interstitial nephritis, chronic renal failure in survivors.
- **"Accelerated" nephrotoxicity** — *Amanita proxima* (Europe), *A. smithiana* (North America): acute renal failure within 1–3 days, **without** significant hepatitis.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** — *Tricholoma equestre* (the man-on-horseback): muscle breakdown → myoglobinuria → kidneys.
- Rare additions: *Psilocybe cubensis* by mass spectrometry — an uncommon cause of acute renal insufficiency.

**Management:** hemodialysis is often indicated (with orellanine the toxin barely clears); kidney transplantation is the fallback for irreversible injury.

## Author's conclusions

The clinician should suspect a nephrotoxic mushroom in sudden renal failure after a "safe" forage: onset timing (days versus weeks) helps separate amanita from cortinarius.

## Worth noting

The review completes Diaz-2005's syndromic scheme with "delayed" and "accelerated" renal failure — and explains why our webcap cards carry "poisonous" status even for species some guides call "conditionally edible": with orellanine there is no such thing as conditional edibility.
