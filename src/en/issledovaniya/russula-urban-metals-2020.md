Chemosphere 2020. Elemental composition of Russula cyanoxantha along an urbanization gradient in Cluj-Napoca, Romania (PMID 31446272).

## Why this study

Our wine-cap card warns "don't eat mushrooms off someone else's mulch". This study tests the intuition "city mushroom = dirty mushroom" on real data — and finds a more layered picture.

## Methods

- *R. cyanoxantha* and soil samples from **6 forests along an urbanisation gradient** + 2 city parks (Cluj-Napoca).
- Elemental analysis (ICP-OES): Cd, Cr, Fe, Mn, Ti and others.
- Accumulation matched against **soil properties**, not just "city vs forest".

## Findings — counter-intuitive

- **Cadmium: forest specimens (3.15–14.1 mg/kg) held MORE than urban ones (0.39 mg/kg).**
- Fe and Mn — higher in forest; Cr and Ti — higher in city.
- The key conclusion: accumulation is set by the **specific soil** (pH, geology, mushroom species), not a simple "city/forest" binary.

## Why it matters

A correction to the popular myth "city mushrooms are toxic, forest ones clean": on a concrete species it is shown that **geology and soil matter more than an administrative boundary**. Practical lessons:

- You cannot judge a mushroom's cleanliness "from a map" — only from analysing the local substrate.
- Edible species accumulate cadmium **even in forests** — the general thesis of our "metals in mushrooms" line (see `caperatus-mercury-falandysz-2014`).
- A garden mushroom on clean home mulch can be cleaner than a "wild forest" one from a cadmium soil — and vice versa.
