## What maitake is

**Maitake** (*Grifola frondosa*) is an edible polypore from the Meripilaceae family, widespread in temperate forests of East Asia, Europe and North America. In Japan the name "maitake" ("dancing mushroom") is explained by a legend: foragers were so happy to find it that they danced. The botanical species card is in [the catalog](/en/griby/maitake/).

The fungus forms characteristic cascading fruiting bodies of many fan-shaped caps fused at the base. It is eaten (sautéed, soups) and used in the folk medicine of China and Japan as a general strengthening and adaptogenic remedy. Since the late 20th century maitake has been a subject of intensive research into bioactive polysaccharides.

## Bioactive substances

Modern literature highlights several key groups of compounds:

- **Beta-glucans** — β-(1→3)/(1→6)-glucans, common to many fungi (cf. the "Beta-glucans and immunity" article). This fraction accounts for the main immunomodulating effects.
- **D-fraction** — an isolated proteoglycan complex of ~1000 kDa molecular mass that underlies most clinical work on maitake. Registered as a nutraceutical in Japan and the US.
- **MD-fraction** — a fraction enriched in smaller glucan fragments, studied in combination with vitamin C for better bioavailability.
- **Ergosterol** and its derivatives — vitamin D₂ precursors.
- **Antioxidants** — phenolic compounds, flavonoids, enzymes (superoxide dismutase, catalase).

## Immunological effects

Maitake's immunomodulating action is the most reproducible group of results:

- In **in-vitro preclinical models**, D-fraction activates macrophages, NK cells, cytotoxic T-lymphocytes and dendritic cells via Toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR4) and the Dectin-1 receptor.
- In **animal models**, maitake extracts increase antitumour activity in synergy with chemotherapy (carcinoma, sarcoma, leukaemia models).
- In **Phase I/II clinical trials**, purified D-fraction (n=34) increased the functional activity of blood granulocytes and monocytes in oncology patients, confirming an immunomodulating effect in humans. Details in the card [Maitake: D-fraction and immunity](/en/issledovaniya/maitake-dfraction-2009/).

## Metabolic effects

The review by Michael (2025) and earlier work summarize these directions:

- **Lipid profile.** In small RCTs in people with mild hypercholesterolemia, maitake extracts lowered total cholesterol, LDL and triglycerides without significantly affecting HDL. The effect is attributed to β-glucans binding bile acids in the gut.
- **Carbohydrate metabolism.** In animal models of type 2 diabetes, reduced postprandial glycemia and improved insulin sensitivity were recorded. Human data are limited and heterogeneous.
- **Body weight.** In small studies, maitake extracts combined with acidic polysaccharides showed a moderate effect on body-mass index (BMI) — the evidence quality is still low.

## Oncology indications (adjuvant therapy)

Maitake is one of the most studied fungi in adjuvant oncology. In Japan, D-fraction has historically been used alongside standard therapy for breast, lung and liver cancer. The 5-year survival synthesis is in [Mushroom supplements in integrative oncology](/en/stati/gribnye-dobavki-onkologiya/). More on PSK/PSP from the related Trametes species in [PSK and PSP: fungal polysaccharides in oncology](/en/stati/trametes-psk-psp/).

Important: the reviews by Michael (2025) and Mohammed et al. (2026) stress that **the quality of clinical evidence for maitake remains moderate** — most studies are pilots with small samples. Adequately designed Phase III RCTs are extremely rare.

## Neuroprotection

A separate line of work (mostly preclinical) covers the neuroprotective properties of grifola: in rodent models of Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, maitake extracts reduced neuroinflammation and improved cognitive measures. Translation of these data to humans is not yet confirmed.

## Culinary use

Maitake is a highly valued culinary mushroom. It keeps its texture during cooking and does not "boil into mush" like many other mushrooms. Used:

- in fried dishes (large-cap "steaks", Asian wok cooking);
- in soups (especially Japanese and Korean traditions);
- in risotto and pasta;
- as pie filling;
- in tempura (Japan).

Maitake does not require prolonged pre-boiling, unlike many medicinal mushrooms. Culinary use does not replace therapeutic use, but delivers part of the β-glucans with food.

## Contraindications and precautions

- **Allergy.** Hypersensitivity reactions are possible in susceptible people.
- **Autoimmune diseases.** Because of its immunostimulating action, maitake should be used cautiously in systemic lupus, rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis (theoretical risk of exacerbation).
- **Anticoagulants.** β-glucans in high doses may potentiate warfarin and aspirin.
- **Pregnancy and lactation.** Adequate safety data are lacking.
- **Before and after surgery.** Given the immunomodulating action, a 2-week pause before a planned surgical procedure is advisable.

## Conclusion

Maitake occupies a middle ground between classic medicinal mushrooms (reishi, trametes) and everyday culinary fungi. The evidence base is "moderately encouraging" for immunomodulation and lipid profile, and "preliminary" for oncology. It fits well as a component of a balanced diet, but not as a standalone treatment for serious diseases.
